# Grounding research: human code review vs. agentic code review, for a bespoke FN review agent

**Research question:** What do the best human reviewers (principal/staff engineers, architects) actually do when they review code, and what does the current state of the art in agentic code review look like — including the transparency-vs-noise tradeoff — so Chuck can ground ideation on a bespoke Field Nation code review agent aimed at reducing human review to a minimum?

**Date:** 2026-08-05

**Scope:** Two parallel tracks. Track 1 covers human review practice from a principal-engineer/architect lens: what they prioritize, review philosophy, how they scale architectural judgment beyond the diff, how they calibrate effort, comment style, anti-patterns, and quality metrics. Track 2 covers agentic code review: the current tool landscape (Gitar, CodeRabbit, PR-Agent/Qodo, Copilot review, Cody, Graphite, CodeGuru, Greptile), the transparency-vs-noise question specifically (directly relevant to the live disagreement with Sonar/Gitar), architectural patterns for building a reviewer agent, what "reducing human review to a minimum" actually takes, and known failure modes. Excluded: FN-specific implementation design (that's the next conversation) and a formal vendor bake-off (Gitar evaluation continues separately, tracked in `knowledge/tech-context.md`).

---

## Summary

The two tracks converge on the same underlying idea from opposite directions. Human review research shows that what separates a principal-engineer-level review from a junior one isn't more scrutiny of the diff — it's judgment that comes from context the diff doesn't contain: design fit, blast radius, architectural consistency, and calibrated effort (skim vs. thorough) based on risk. Google's own review-philosophy doc, a large Microsoft Research empirical study, and the Cisco/SmartBear study all converge on the same practical rules: review small changes carefully, escalate scrutiny with risk and blast radius, separate blocking issues from nits, and optimize for code health over time rather than gate-passing. Three independent studies also converge on one hard number worth building into any tooling: review quality degrades once a changeset exceeds roughly 200–400 lines or touches many files — smaller diffs get better review, from humans or agents alike.

Agentic review research shows the field arrived at almost the identical set of conclusions from the tool-building side, but is actively split on transparency. Sonar's Gitar deliberately withholds "how I reviewed this" to stay low-noise; CodeRabbit ships the opposite bet — an explicit, explainable walkthrough — and both are live in the market today. The literature (human-AI trust calibration, automation bias research from radiology and aviation, and one directly-on-point 2026 academic vision paper on agentic code review) says this isn't a wash: exposing process is necessary for a human to calibrate reliance correctly, but exposing everything creates the same noise problem Gitar is trying to solve. The resolution both the literature and the more sophisticated vendors have landed on is a layered artifact — a low-noise inline signal plus a separate, persistent "what I checked, what I judged fine, my confidence" record — not a binary choice between noise and silence. That reframes Chuck's disagreement with Gitar more precisely: it isn't that Gitar has no transparency mechanism (its dashboard comment is architecturally the right shape), it's that Gitar scopes that artifact's content too narrowly.

On the specific goal of reducing human review to a minimum, the strongest real-world evidence is Meta's RADAR system: a risk-stratified funnel (eligibility gates → risk score → LLM review → deterministic validation) that auto-lands a meaningful share of low-risk diffs with materially lower downstream incident rates than the normal path — but it explicitly does not eliminate human review generally, only for changes that clear a configurable risk bar. Every credible source agrees current AI reviewers still miss the review category humans are hardest to replace on: "technically correct but the wrong thing to build," which requires product/business context no diff-level (or even repo-level) analysis captures. The honest framing for Chuck's agent, then, is not "build a reviewer good enough that nobody checks it" but "build a risk-tiered gate that earns the right to skip human review incrementally, with a transparency artifact good enough that when a human does look, they actually know what was and wasn't covered."

## Findings

### Track 1: How principal-engineer-level reviewers actually review code

**The priority hierarchy senior reviewers use.** Google's [eng-practices](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/looking-for.html) documentation gives the most explicit and most cited ordering: design first (does this belong here, does it fit the system, is now the right time), then functionality (does it do what's intended, for both end users and future maintainers), then complexity (with an explicit warning against speculative over-engineering), then tests, then naming/comments/style/documentation last. This is the inverse of where inexperienced reviewers instinctively spend attention — style and naming are cheap to spot and comment on, while design and architectural fit require holding the whole system in your head. Gergely Orosz's ["Good Code Reviews, Better Code Reviews"](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/good-code-reviews-better-code-reviews/) frames this as a maturity spectrum: "good" reviews evaluate the diff on its own terms (correctness, tests, obvious issues); "better" reviews additionally ask whether the change fits the larger system, whether it needs to exist at all, and what it costs to maintain long-term — essentially, does this diff make the *system* better, not just "is this diff correct."

Palantir's [engineering guide](https://medium.com/palantir/code-review-best-practices-19e02780015f) adds concrete triage heuristics: scrutinize changes to dependencies/build system heavily (they affect coupling org-wide), flag anything breaking backward compatibility (schema, public API, user workflow), and separate refactors from behavior changes so expensive human attention isn't spent on style debates while real logic changes hide inside a large diff. The empirical [Microsoft Research study](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/bosu2015useful.pdf) (1.5M review comments at Microsoft) validates this from the author's side: comments on functional defects and corner cases were rated most useful; nitpicks (indentation, naming, typos) were only "somewhat useful"; pure praise or pure clarifying questions were rated *not* useful by authors (though praise still has social value).

**Review philosophy.** Google's core standard: favor approving once a change *improves* code health, even if imperfect — "there is no such thing as perfect code, only better code." Block only if a change *degrades* code health, not for imperfection. GitHub staff engineer Sarah Vessels' [philosophy](https://github.blog/developer-skills/github/how-to-review-code-effectively-a-github-staff-engineers-philosophy/) reframes the reviewer's role: the author has the most context, so the reviewer's job is to ask questions and offer evidence (a test, a query, a graph) rather than assert corrections. She treats "Request Changes" as a heavy tool reserved for near-certain production/security risk, defaulting to approve-with-comments otherwise. Martin Fowler's structural critique is that PR-gated review is only one mechanism, and arguably not the best one — his ["Refinement Code Review"](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/RefinementCodeReview.html) argues continuous, ongoing refinement (pairing, small cleanups) does more for code health than a single merge-time checkpoint. Kent Beck's *Tidy First?* echoes Palantir on separating structural "tidyings" from behavior changes, going as far as suggesting tidyings might not need review at all, being lower-risk by construction.

**Architectural/systemic review is structurally different from diff-level review, and this is the biggest gap for tooling to close.** One industry analysis states it plainly: architectural coherence "is best enforced through design documents, ADRs, and dedicated architecture reviews, not through per-commit diff inspection," because no single diff shows the cumulative effect of many small changes. A useful escalation heuristic surfaced in this research: *if this behavior changed silently, how wide would the blast radius be, and how long until someone noticed?* — framing "needs deeper review" as a property of a failure's invisibility and reach, not the diff's size. Architecture debt specifically "is not visible in a pull request and doesn't appear as a broken test or a scanner finding" — a diff-level reviewer is structurally blind to it unless they're independently tracking the system's shape. This is exactly the tacit knowledge a principal engineer supplies and that an agent needs an explicit substitute for (ADR/design-doc retrieval, dependency-graph awareness, cross-repo consistency checks) if it's meant to replicate that judgment rather than re-deriving a diff-level checklist.

**How reviewers calibrate effort — the best-evidenced section of this track.** The [Cisco/SmartBear study](https://static1.smartbear.co/support/media/resources/cc/11_best_practices_for_peer_code_review_redirected.pdf) (2,500 reviews, 3.2M LOC, 10 months) gives concrete numbers: review 200–400 LOC at a time, stay under 300–500 LOC/hour, cap sessions at 60–90 minutes (defect-finding drops off after that), and never skip review even on a one-line change (a single line can have system-wide consequences). Done this way, review catches 70–90% of existing defects. The same study found that when authors annotate their own diff explaining their reasoning before review, defect density drops sharply — confirmed via manual audit to be a real reduction in bugs, not reviewer complacency, because the act of explaining forces authors to re-examine their own work. Staff engineer Gustav Wengel's [explicit thoroughness taxonomy](https://www.gustavwengel.dk/how-thoroughly-should-you-review-pull-requests) — comments-only, skimming, regular, thorough — is the clearest description of *conscious* effort modulation: thorough review (actively trying to provoke edge cases/exploits, cross-subsystem impact, performance benchmarking) is reserved for critical/security code; most real-world review lands at skimming or regular. The Microsoft Research study found reviewer familiarity with a file roughly doubles comment usefulness (32–37% for first-timers vs. 65–71% for repeat reviewers of that file), and — counter-intuitively — cross-team reviewers gave slightly *more* useful comments than same-team reviewers across all five projects studied. Palantir's numeric rule of thumb: split a change if it touches more than ~5 files, took more than 1–2 days to write, or would take more than 20 minutes to review.

**Comment style and communication.** The strongest convergence in the whole track: favor questions and evidence over directives, and explicitly label blocking vs. non-blocking feedback. Google's "Nit:" prefix convention is the origin point for a now-universal pattern; the [Conventional Comments](https://conventionalcomments.org/) standard formalizes this further with labels (`praise`, `nitpick`, `suggestion`, `issue`, `question`, `thought`, `chore`) plus an optional `(non-blocking)` tag, letting an author triage feedback by scanning labels rather than parsing tone — directly relevant to Chuck's transparency question, since an agent that enforces this taxonomy explicitly resolves a lot of the ambiguity at stake in the Gitar disagreement. Vessels' before/after examples are concrete and trainable: "I don't like this" → "This line is doing a lot, could we simplify it to improve readability?"; "This won't work" → "This won't work because [X], see this relevant issue: [link]." Palantir adds explicit differentiation of suggestions, required changes, and open discussion points, and recommends stating exactly how much response is expected before merge ("feel free to merge after the minor suggestions" vs. "let me know when I can take another look").

**What good review is not.** Documented anti-patterns worth building an agent to actively avoid: rubber-stamping/LGTM culture (approvals within minutes, no substantive comments, no evidence of actually reading the diff); AWS's [catalog](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/de_de/wellarchitected/latest/devops-guidance/anti-patterns-for-code-review.html) of infrequent reviews, excessive required reviewers (bottleneck, not thoroughness), large batch reviews, hostile tone, and lack of follow-through on findings. A 2024 academic paper on [debiasing code review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.01407) documents confirmation bias (under time pressure, reviewers search for a fast path to approval rather than objectively verifying), availability bias (prior visible comments prime what a later reviewer notices), and decision fatigue (judgment degrades over high-volume review sessions, especially late in the day). Separately, [anchoring bias research](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1707.03869) found that displaying an arbitrary comprehensibility score measurably anchored reviewers' subjective quality judgments — directly relevant if Chuck's agent surfaces a confidence or severity score, since that number itself becomes an anchor for the human reviewing it. The Cisco study's "Big Brother" finding: using defect density as an individual performance metric backfires (developers game the metric, and the most experienced engineers often handle the hardest, most error-prone code, so raw defect counts are a poor skill proxy) — management should commit that these numbers are never used against individuals.

**Metrics.** Turnaround/latency metrics are the most commonly tracked and least sufficient on their own. Defect escape rate is the most direct outcome metric but lags by definition. The Microsoft Research paper's "comment usefulness density" metric is the most rigorously validated way to measure review quality beyond activity counts (they built an automated classifier at 85–90% precision/recall against human-labeled ground truth). A useful and current warning: DORA metrics were built on an assumption — human-authored code, review capacity matched to output — that AI-assisted authorship is breaking; one industry telemetry report found high-AI-adoption teams merged 98% more PRs with review time up 91% and PR size up 154%, i.e., the review bottleneck didn't shrink with AI-assisted authorship, it got worse (this specific figure is reported via secondary sources, not independently verified — treat as directional). Three independent studies (Cisco's 200–400 LOC line, Microsoft Research's file-count effect, and one industry "elite teams keep PRs under ~194 lines" figure) converge on the same conclusion from entirely different data: smaller changesets get measurably better review, whoever or whatever is reviewing them.

*Confidence note on Track 1:* The strongest material is Google's eng-practices docs, the Microsoft Research study, the Cisco/SmartBear study, and the cognitive-bias arXiv paper — all read directly, not summarized secondhand. Material on Will Larson's *Staff Engineer* and *The Art of Readable Code* is secondhand (blog summaries, not the primary text) — worth pulling the actual books if this matters for the final design. Several frequently-repeated industry statistics (rubber-stamp rates, specific velocity-improvement percentages) could not be traced to a verifiable primary source and are flagged as industry lore rather than fact.

### Track 2: Agentic code review — state of the art, transparency, and architecture

**The tool landscape.** [Gitar](https://gitar.ai/blog/ai-code-review-without-the-comment-spam) (Sonar, acquired mid-2026, built by ex-Uber platform engineers) claims full-codebase awareness and iterates fixes against CI until green; its explicit design thesis is that AI reviewers "spam" PRs with low-signal comments developers stop reading, so it consolidates everything into a single, continuously-updated dashboard comment rather than a trail of inline remarks. All of Gitar's performance claims trace back to Sonar's own materials — no independent benchmark was found. [CodeRabbit](https://docs.coderabbit.ai/overview/architecture) is the most heavily documented architecture in the market: sandboxed full-repo clone, 50+ static analyzers run alongside the LLM, agentic exploration via shell tools (`grep`, `ast-grep`, `git diff`) rather than fixed retrieval, specialized parallel agents (Review, Verification, Chat, Pre-Merge, Finishing Touches), and a persistent "Learnings" memory of past feedback and conventions. Notably, CodeRabbit markets the *opposite* stance from Gitar on transparency — an explicit, dependency-ordered "walkthrough" narrating what the model read and how it understood the change, not just a list of complaints — making it a live, in-market counter-example to Gitar's philosophy from a competitor with real (if vendor-reported) traction.

[PR-Agent/Qodo](https://deepwiki.com/qodo-ai/pr-agent) is the open-source predecessor, now community-governed, with a layered orchestration architecture and multi-model support. Qodo's own engineering blog contains one of the most useful non-marketing admissions found in this research: they built a state-of-the-art RAG/codebase-indexing system for review, then **substantially tore it out**, explaining that once agents got good at exploring a repo directly (grep/git-diff-style, the way a human actually investigates a PR), the marginal lift from a maintained vector index shrank toward zero relative to its infrastructure cost. They repurposed retrieval specifically for what an agent *can't* rediscover from the working tree — past review decisions, recurring feedback, team conventions living in PR history rather than a style guide ([source](https://www.qodo.ai/blog/we-built-a-state-of-the-art-rag-system-for-code-review-in-qodo-2-4-we-took-most-of-it-out/)).

GitHub Copilot code review (GA April 2025, agentic overhaul March 2026) explores the repo and traces cross-file dependencies, but has a documented quirk — it ignores existing PR description content and works best against blank descriptions — and now consumes a 13x premium-request multiplier per review, a real cost consideration at scale. Sourcegraph Cody repositioned enterprise-only in 2025; independent (unverified) commentary says it struggles with complex multi-step logic and long dependency chains despite strong codebase-aware retrieval. Graphite's "Diamond" reviewer launched in 2025 with the explicit public stance that "AI will never replace human code review" — notably different from Chuck's stated end goal. Amazon's CodeGuru Reviewer (ML-based static analysis) is being sunset (no new repo associations since November 2025) in favor of the general Q Developer assistant — evidence that the standalone "classic ML + static analysis" reviewer category is consolidating into LLM/agentic assistants industry-wide. Greptile differentiates on full-codebase semantic-graph indexing and claims an 82% bug-catch rate, but an independent re-evaluation reportedly found real-world detection closer to ~45% on the same repos with materially more false positives than CodeRabbit — a useful caution that none of the vendor accuracy claims in this space (including Gitar's and CodeRabbit's) come from a peer-reviewed or vendor-neutral benchmark; treat all specific percentages across the whole tool landscape as directional, not verified.

**The transparency-vs-noise tradeoff.** This is the crux of the disagreement with Gitar, and there's real literature behind it — mostly from human-AI trust-calibration and automation-bias research applied to other domains, plus one directly-on-point 2026 SE paper. The core finding is a genuine tension, not a simple "more transparency wins" story: the goal in trust-calibration research is reliance that tracks actual system capability, and **explanations don't automatically fix miscalibration** — multiple studies note explanations can increase reliance even when trust isn't correspondingly warranted, a form of automation bias ([AI & Society, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-025-02422-7)). This is a real counterpoint worth having ready: a transparent-but-wrong summary can still produce misplaced confidence, so transparency is necessary but not sufficient.

On the specific "does showing reasoning help or just add noise" question, the XAI literature converges on a "transparency paradox": too little detail leaves reviewers guessing (Chuck's exact complaint about Gitar), too much causes overload and can *decrease* trust and usability. The most consistently recommended resolution is **layered explanation** — a low-noise, always-visible summary plus deeper detail available on demand — which both the literature and the more sophisticated vendors have converged on independently. CodeRabbit's walkthrough comment is this pattern in production; a 2026 academic vision paper, ["Rethinking Code Review in the Age of AI"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17548), proposes a formal architecture with a dedicated "Review Summary Generation Agent" and persistent "Review Metrics Storage" for exactly this reason, explicitly naming automation bias and transparency among its open challenges, and citing that automated repair specifically "requires developer trust and auditability, necessitating clear signals and standard review transparency."

This reframes the Gitar disagreement more precisely than "Gitar has no transparency": Gitar's single consolidated dashboard comment is architecturally *close* to the pattern the literature recommends (one persistent artifact rather than an inline comment trail) — the actual gap is that Gitar deliberately scopes that artifact's content to exclude "what I checked and judged fine," where CodeRabbit's walkthrough explicitly tries to narrate that. Cross-domain evidence backs the "you can't skip visibility" side of the argument: radiology research found false-positive AI flags led inexperienced readers to recommend more aggressive follow-up than warranted (anchoring on confident-looking AI output even when wrong); aviation NTSB investigations found pilots over-relying on automation they didn't fully understand, eroding their own skills over time. The mitigation pattern in both domains is structured acknowledgment — checklists forcing positive confirmation of specific dimensions (for review: what was and wasn't checked) — which supports the idea that omitting "what I checked and judged fine" is actively counter to safe automation design, not just a UX preference.

**Architectural patterns worth building on.** Multi-agent specialization, not a single monolithic prompt, is the dominant pattern: an orchestrator routing to specialized sub-agents (correctness, security, performance, style/design, test coverage), plus — notably in the academic vision paper — dedicated agents that police the *quality of the review comments themselves* (toxicity, usefulness), not just the code. Static analysis combined with LLM review, not LLM alone, is the consistent pattern across CodeRabbit and academic work; one study combining LLMs with static bug detectors to cut false positives got to ~0.93–0.94 accuracy but recall stayed below the ~90% threshold enterprises expect, meaning manual review remains a necessary backstop even with a good hybrid pipeline. On context retrieval, the field's consensus has shifted decisively away from pre-built RAG/vector indexes toward agentic, on-demand exploration (grep/git/AST tools) for the diff-adjacent investigation, reserving a lighter persistent memory specifically for what can't be rediscovered from the working tree — past review decisions, recurring feedback, conventions. This is directly actionable for Chuck's design: don't build a full-repo vector index as the primary context mechanism; give the agent tool-based repo access for in-the-moment investigation, and a separate durable memory store for cross-PR history and overrides — which also happens to be exactly the substrate needed for the transparency-artifact pattern above. On confidence/self-critique, verbalized LLM confidence is a real (if imperfect) signal usable to gate action — auto-apply only above a threshold, route low-confidence findings to humans — and adversarial patterns (one agent proposes, a separate critic agent filters before surfacing) are a concrete way to cut false positives. The academic paper flags governance questions worth deciding up front rather than retrofitting: retention/decay policy for review memory, who approves behavior changes derived from it, and privacy implications of storing reviewer/codebase data indefinitely.

**What "reducing human review to a minimum" actually requires.** The strongest real-world evidence is Meta's [RADAR system](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30208) (2026, Meta-authored, arXiv): a multi-stage funnel — eligibility gates by change type → static heuristics → a machine-learned Diff Risk Score used as a *gating* mechanism (not just an advisory score) → LLM-based review → deterministic validation before landing without human review. Reported figures (search-summarized, not independently verified against the full paper): 535K+ diffs processed through the funnel, 331K+ landed via automation, with incident rates on automated diffs roughly 1/50th of the non-automated path, and widening the automation threshold increased yield without degrading safety outcomes. This directly reframes Chuck's goal: the organizations actually succeeding at "skip human review for most PRs" do it through **risk stratification**, not a single smarter reviewer — low-risk changes auto-land after AI review plus deterministic checks, higher-risk changes still route to humans. Critically, RADAR does not claim to eliminate human review generally, only for changes clearing a configurable risk bar.

Credible skepticism tempers ambition here. A comparative academic evaluation found a 92% F1 degradation when LLM reviewers were tested on real, uncurated PRs instead of curated benchmarks, with diff size as the dominant confound — strong evidence that lab accuracy numbers (including every vendor claim in the tool-landscape section) likely overstate real-world performance. The same study found a smaller model (Claude Haiku) outperforming a larger one (Claude Sonnet) on this task, undercutting a naive "bigger model wins" assumption worth testing empirically for whatever model Chuck picks. Independent commentary converges on a structural limitation no architecture here solves: AI reviewers miss "technically correct but the wrong thing to build" issues, which require product/business context that diff-level or even repo-level analysis doesn't have — at best, surfacing linked ticket/issue context narrows this gap, it doesn't close it. A widely-cited (unverified primary source) 2026 survey figure claims 66% of engineers refuse to merge without manual review regardless of AI review results, with trust in AI accuracy reportedly dropping year-over-year — directionally consistent with everything else: today's real-world adoption is "AI review plus mandatory human review," not "instead of." A related headwind worth flagging: as FN's own engineers increasingly use Claude Code/Copilot to write code, several sources report AI-generated code trending toward *more*, not fewer, logic and security issues than human-written code — meaning the review target itself may be drifting toward the profile current AI reviewers are worst at catching reliably, independent of how good the reviewer agent is.

**Known failure modes.** Comment spam is real and quantified (if imprecisely) — aggregator sources cite 5–15% typical false-positive rates and estimates of dozens of hours per month spent filtering AI review noise on a mid-sized team; this is the empirical backing for the problem Gitar says it's solving, and the disagreement with Chuck is about the cure, not whether the problem exists. Hallucinated findings cut both ways — one study found roughly half of an LLM "hallucination judge"'s flags were themselves false positives, while a deterministic AST-analysis approach hit 100% precision with zero false positives, suggesting hybrid symbolic+LLM verification is currently more reliable than LLM-only self-checking. A documented real-world incident (reported, not independently verified): an AWS engineer using an agentic coding tool with operator-level permissions to fix a small bug triggered a reported 13-hour outage when the agent deleted and rebuilt a production environment — a permissions/blast-radius failure directly relevant to how much write authority any fixer-agent should get, reinforcing RADAR's "deterministic validation before landing" gate. The most consistently repeated non-vendor critique across this whole track: current AI reviewers surface more low-severity nitpicks without a corresponding increase in high-severity defect catches — the opposite of what "reduce human review" actually needs, since it's the high-stakes findings that justify skipping a human check, not the easy ones.

*Confidence note on Track 2:* the strongest, most independently credible sources are the Meta RADAR paper, the "Rethinking Code Review" vision paper, the comparative LLM evaluation paper, the human-factors/XAI trust-calibration literature, and Qodo's RAG-retraction blog post (a rare vendor source disclosing a negative result about their own product). A meaningful share of the tool-comparison figures (bug-catch rates, false-positive counts, adoption/trust survey percentages) trace to AI-code-review-comparison aggregator/listicle sites rather than primary disclosures or peer-reviewed benchmarks — flagged inline above; treat specific percentages from those sources as directional only.

### What this means for Field Nation's bespoke agent (synthesis, not a design)

A few threads worth carrying into the design conversation, drawn directly from where both tracks land on the same point:

- **The Gitar disagreement has a sharper, more defensible framing than "no transparency."** Gitar's single dashboard comment is architecturally the right shape (a persistent artifact, not a comment trail); the actual gap is that it deliberately excludes "what I checked and judged fine." Both the trust-calibration literature and CodeRabbit's competing product bet support building that content in, in a layered/low-noise way, rather than treating transparency and noise-reduction as opposites.
- **"Reduce human review to a minimum" is a triage/gating architecture problem, not a pure model-accuracy problem.** RADAR's risk-stratified funnel is the closest real precedent to what Chuck is trying to do, and it gives a defensible incremental rollout path — start with a narrow low-risk eligibility gate, widen it as false-negative data accumulates — rather than an all-or-nothing bet on reviewer quality.
- **Context architecture should favor agentic exploration over a maintained index**, per Qodo's own retraction of their RAG system — grep/git/AST tool access for in-the-moment investigation, plus a separate, durable memory specifically for cross-PR history, team conventions, and past human overrides (which doubles as the substrate for both the learning loop and the transparency artifact).
- **Human review research supplies a ready-made priority hierarchy and effort-calibration model an agent can be evaluated against**, not just inspired by: Google's design-first ordering, the Cisco 200–400 LOC / 60–90 minute calibration numbers, and Wengel's four-tier thoroughness model all give concrete, testable benchmarks for whether the agent's behavior actually matches what a principal engineer does, rather than a vague aspiration to "review like an expert."
- **The hardest category to close — "technically correct but the wrong thing to build" — is a structural gap, not a maturity gap.** No amount of better prompting solves this without product/business context (linked tickets, specs, ADRs) as explicit agent inputs, which argues for treating this as a first-class design requirement rather than an edge case to accept.

## Key uncertainties

- Several frequently-repeated industry statistics in both tracks (rubber-stamp rates, specific velocity-improvement percentages, AI-review false-positive/trust-survey figures) could not be traced to a verifiable primary source in this research pass and are flagged as directional industry lore rather than settled fact.
- The Meta RADAR paper's headline figures (535K+ diffs, 1/50th incident rate) were captured from search summaries, not the full paper text — worth pulling and reading arXiv:2605.30208 directly before citing these numbers anywhere more formal than internal ideation.
- Hindsight bias specifically in code review (distinct from post-incident review) appears to be a genuine gap in the literature, not a search failure — an open question rather than something answered elsewhere and missed.
- No independent, vendor-neutral benchmark of Gitar's, CodeRabbit's, or Greptile's actual accuracy/false-positive rates exists in what was found — every specific percentage in the tool-landscape section should be treated as a starting hypothesis to test against Field Nation's own repos, not a fact to design around.
- Whether Chuck's specific model choice (per the comparative evaluation study, "bigger isn't always better" for this task) holds for whatever model FN ultimately picks is untested here and worth an internal eval before committing.
